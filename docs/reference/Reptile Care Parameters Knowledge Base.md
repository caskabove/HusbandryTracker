# Reptile Care Parameters Knowledge Base

Oct 29, 2026 · @Kevin

Standard husbandry settings for the 10 most commonly kept pet snakes and 10 most commonly kept pet lizards: temperature zones, humidity, and how often and how much to feed. Ranges are the consensus of current care sheets (ReptiFiles, Reptiles Magazine, PetMD, vet-practice handouts); where sources disagree, the range given covers the mainstream values.

**How to read the tables**

- Temperatures are °F. *Basking* is the surface temperature under the heat source (measure with an IR thermometer). *Cool* is the cool-end air. *Night* is the acceptable night-time low.
- Humidity is ambient relative humidity. "Humid hide" means a hide with damp moss as a local high-humidity spot.
- Feeding is split by life stage. Snake prey size is "about as wide as the snake's thickest point" unless noted.
- These are starting points for a healthy, captive-bred animal. Individual animals, localities and breeding/brumation cycles shift them.

For Husbandry Tracker, these ranges can seed the default plausible ranges and per-species targets in the metric registry (RHA-57).

## Snakes: heat and humidity

Most popular pet snakes want a 85–95 °F warm spot and a 70s °F cool end; the big split is humidity, from arid species (hognose, rosy boa, sand boa, gopher) at 30–50% to tropical boids (ball python, boa) at 50–70%.

## Snakes: feeding

All ten eat whole rodents, frozen/thawed preferred; feeding intervals stretch from about weekly for babies to every 2–4 weeks for adult boids. Prey should be about as wide as the snake's thickest point (roughly 10% of body weight) unless noted.

| Common name | Babies / juveniles | Adults | Typical adult prey | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Ball python | 1 prey every 5–7 days | Every 2–4 weeks (males often 2–3, females 3–4) | Small to medium rat | Fasting for weeks to months is common, especially in winter; overfeeding is the bigger risk. |
| Corn snake | Pinky every 5–7 days | Every 10–21 days | Adult mouse or rat fuzzy | Reliable feeder. Move to rat pups for large adults. |
| California kingsnake | Every 5–7 days | Every 7–14 days | Adult mouse, small rat | Strong feeding response; snake-eating, so never cohabit. Watch for obesity. |
| Western hognose snake | Pinky every 5–7 days | Every 10–14 days | Small to adult mouse | Prey up to 1.5× body width. Some hatchlings need scenting (fish or lizard) to start. |
| Boa constrictor | Every 7–10 days | Every 2–4 weeks | Medium to large rat; rabbit for big females | Large prey spacing avoids obesity, a common captive problem. |
| Honduran milk snake | Every 5–7 days | Every 7–14 days | Adult mouse, small rat | Feeding refusals usually trace to a warm side running too cool. |
| Rosy boa | Every 7–10 days | Every 10–21 days | Small to adult mouse | Rarely large enough for rats. May fast in winter. |
| Kenyan sand boa | Pinky every 5–7 days | Every 2–4 weeks (males eat smaller prey) | Fuzzy to adult mouse | Ambushes from under the sand; feed with long tongs. |
| Gopher snake / bullsnake | Every 5–7 days | Every 7–14 days | Adult mouse to small/medium rat | Large bullsnakes take rats; babies start on mice. |
| Carpet python | Every 7–10 days | Every 2–3 weeks | Small to medium rat | Strong feeding response; tap-train before handling on feeding days. |

## Lizards: heat and humidity

Lizards vary far more than snakes: desert baskers (bearded dragon, uromastyx, tegu, iguana) need 105–135 °F basking surfaces, while the New Caledonian geckos (crested, gargoyle) are stressed above about 82–85 °F and need no basking lamp at all. Basking figures here are surface temperatures; diurnal species also need UVB.

The biggest disagreements between sources are basking temperatures for tegus and iguanas (older sheets say 95–100 °F air, newer ReptiFiles sheets say 120–135 °F surface). Both can be true because air and surface readings differ.

## Lizards: feeding

Young lizards eat daily; most adults drop to every other day or a few times a week. Insects should be no larger than the space between the lizard's eyes, gut-loaded 24 hours ahead and dusted with calcium (and periodically a multivitamin).

| Common name | Diet type | Babies / juveniles | Adults | How much | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Bearded dragon | Omnivore (shifts from insects to plants with age) | Insects 2–3× daily under 3 months, then 1× daily; fresh salad daily | Salad daily; insects 2–3× per week | Young: all insects eaten in 10–15 min. Adults: diet \~80% greens/veg, \~20% insects | Overfeeding insects to adults causes obesity and fatty liver. |
| Leopard gecko | Insectivore | Daily | Every 2–3 days | 5–7 appropriately sized insects per feeding for juveniles; adults about 2 insects per inch of body length | Mealworms, waxworms and superworms as treats; feed in the evening. |
| Crested gecko | Omnivore (fruit and insects) | Complete diet (CGD) daily; insects 2× per week | CGD every other day (3–4× per week); insects 1–2× per week | CGD mixed to ketchup consistency in a small cup, removed after 24–48 h | CGD is nutritionally complete; dust insects only. |
| Blue-tongued skink | Omnivore | Daily up to \~3 months, then 3–4× per week | 1–3× per week | Roughly 50% vegetables/greens, 40% animal protein, 10% fruit | Quality dog/cat food is a common protein base; adults are prone to obesity. |
| African fat-tailed gecko | Insectivore | Daily or every other day | 2–3× per week | As many appropriately sized insects as eaten in \~15 min | Plump tail = good body condition; thin tail = underfed or ill. |
| Veiled chameleon | Insectivore (eats some plant matter) | Daily, as many as eaten | Every other day (3–4 days per week) | Adults roughly 3–6 large insects per feeding; gravid females more | Drinks droplets, not from bowls; mist 2–3× daily or use a dripper. |
| Gargoyle gecko | Omnivore (fruit and insects) | CGD daily; insects 2× per week | CGD 3–4× per week; insects 1–2× per week | CGD in a small cup, removed after 24–48 h; occasional pinky for large adults | Takes more insects than crested geckos. |
| Argentine black and white tegu | Omnivore (mostly animal protein) | Daily | 2–3× per week (less in brumation) | Whole prey, lean meat, eggs and insects, plus \~10–20% fruit/veg | Brumates in winter for several months and may stop eating entirely. |
| Green iguana | Herbivore | Daily, 1–2 meals | Daily or every other day | Mostly dark leafy greens and vegetables; fruit 10% or less; no animal protein | Calcium-rich greens (collard, mustard, turnip, dandelion); avoid spinach-heavy diets. |
| Uromastyx | Herbivore | Daily | Daily or \~5× per week | Leafy greens and flowers, plus a small amount of dry seeds/lentils | Water comes mostly from food; keep substrate dry. |

## Notes and sources

- **Species selection.** No authoritative sales ranking exists, so the lists combine the species most often named in popularity lists from Reptiles Magazine, PetMD, Chewy and Reptile Direct. Close runners-up: Baird's rat snake, checkered garter snake, African egg-eating snake; ackie monitor, Chinese water dragon, panther chameleon.
- **Ranges are consensus, not law.** Figures come from the linked care sheets; most were read from the publishers' care-sheet summaries rather than full guides, so verify a value against the full sheet before hard-coding it.
- **Seasonal changes.** Many species brumate or fast in winter (kingsnakes, rosy boas, ball pythons, tegus, bearded dragons). Expect cooler targets and reduced feeding then.
- **Open question for the app.** Several species have two humidity regimes (veiled chameleon day vs. night, crested gecko wet vs. dry, African fat-tailed gecko wet vs. dry season). A single min/max per enclosure would flag these as alerts, so the metric registry may need time-of-day or seasonal targets.
